# Bike fit science and standard protocols: research notes

Compiled 2026-11-03 for the on-device AI bike fit app spec. Sources read directly are marked with the URL used. PubMed abstracts were pulled through the NCBI E-utilities API (efetch) because pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov page fetches were blocked; the canonical URL for each is given as https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/PMID/. Where I could only reach a secondary source, I say so. Web search budget ran out near the end; gaps are listed under Open questions.

## 1. Saddle height methods and the evidence for each

### 1.1 Knee flexion angle at bottom dead center (Holmes method, static)
- Origin: Holmes, Pruitt and Whalen, "Lower extremity overuse in bicycling", Clinics in Sports Medicine 1994, vol 13, pp 187 to 203. I could not reach the original. It is cited by Bini 2011, Peveler 2005 to 2012 and Holliday 2021 as the source of the 25 to 35 degree knee flexion target measured statically with a goniometer, pedal at 6 o'clock, rider stationary. Peveler 2012 abstract: "The current accepted method for adjusting saddle height involves the use of a goniometer to adjust saddle height so that a knee angle between 25 and 35 degrees is obtained. This measurement is taken while the cyclist maintains a static position with the pedal at the 6 o'clock position." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22158094/
- Bini, Hume, Croft 2011 systematic review (Sports Med 41(6):463-76): methods are "varied and not well established"; a 5% change in saddle height changed knee kinematics by 35% and moments by 16%; patellofemoral compressive force is inversely related to saddle height; changes under 4% of trochanteric length do not seem to affect injury risk or performance; occurrence of overuse knee pain is 50% in cyclists; recommendation: set saddle height with the knee flexion angle method (25 to 30 degrees) to reduce knee injury risk and minimize oxygen uptake. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21615188/
- Bini and Priego-Quesada 2022 systematic review (J Sports Sci 40(4):386-400), 41 studies: "Strong evidence suggests that saddle height should be configured using dynamic measurements of the knee angle"; moderate evidence that saddle height changes under 4% of leg length give trivial to small changes in lower limb loads and no effect on oxygen uptake or efficiency; limited evidence on supramaximal performance or injury risk. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34706617/
- Peveler 2008 (JSCR 22(4):1355-9), 15 subjects at 70% VO2max: VO2 significantly lower at 25 degree knee angle than at 35 degrees and at 109% inseam; no HR or RPE difference. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18545167/
- Peveler, Pounders, Bishop 2007 (JSCR 21(4):1023-7), 9 cyclists + 18 noncyclists, Wingate: 109% of inseam fell outside the 25 to 35 degree range 63% of the time; 25 degrees gave higher mean power than 109% in those who fell outside the range. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18076230/
- Peveler and Green 2011 (JSCR 25(3):629-33), 11 well-trained males: VO2 44.77 at 25 degrees vs 45.22 at 35 degrees vs 45.98 ml/kg/min at 109%; peak power 1041.55 W at 25 degrees vs 1002.05 W at 109%; 109% fell outside 25 to 35 degrees 73% of the time. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20581695/
- Bini, Hume, Kilding 2014 (Eur J Sport Sci 14(1):44-52), 12 cyclists + 12 triathletes: the "advocated optimal" height was 25 degree knee flexion at 6 o'clock; raising the saddle 5% from preferred increased index of effectiveness 7% in cyclists; triathletes' preferred saddle gave 5% smaller hip angle and 9% greater hip ROM than cyclists. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533494/
- Priego Quesada 2017 (J Sports Sci 35(14):1459-65), 20 cyclists, 45 min at 50% PPO, knee 20/30/40 degrees x trunk 35/45/55 degrees: most comfortable at 30 degree knee flexion "calculated from the offset position or 40 plus or minus 4 degrees of absolute value" (2D with Fonda correction); 40 degree knee flexion with 35 degree trunk was most uncomfortable; upright trunk most comfortable for recreational riders. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27490817/

### 1.2 Hamley and Thomas 109% of inseam (1967)
- Hamley EJ, Thomas V. "Physiological and postural factors in the calibration of the bicycle ergometer", J Physiol 1967, 191, 55 to 57 (page range varies by citing source). Formula per secondary sources: saddle height from pedal spindle (or 1.09 x inseam minus crank length to BB center). I did not reach the original; cited via Bini 2011 and Peveler. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21615188/
- Evidence against as sole method: Peveler 2007 (outside 25 to 35 degree range 63%), Peveler 2011 (73%), Ferrer-Roca 2012 (56.5% of well-trained cyclists outside 106 to 109% of inseam; 106 to 109% "may not ensure an optimal knee flexion (30 to 40 degrees)"; proposed regression gives 108.6 to 110.4% of inseam; R = 0.963). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22190163/

### 1.3 LeMond 0.883 x inseam (BB center to saddle top)
- Attributed to Cyrille Guimard, LeMond's coach, early 1980s; ignores crank length; pedal and shoe stack change the result; a reader comment says LeMond suggested subtracting 3 mm for clipless pedals. RoadBikeRider article by Rick Schultz. https://www.roadbikerider.com/lemond-formula-saddle-height/
- Peveler, Bishop, Smith, Whitehorn 2005 (JEPonline 8(1):51-55), 19 cyclists: LeMond and Hamley did not differ (p = 0.917); heel-toe was significantly lower than Hamley (p = 0.004); LeMond put riders inside 25 to 35 degrees about 65% of the time, heel-toe about 70%; the authors recommend the Holmes method. Abstract read via https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4010655?lng=en ; Active.com summary says Hamley hit the range 67% of the time (secondary): https://www.active.com/cycling/articles/how-to-find-the-right-saddle-height?page=2

### 1.4 Heel method
- Only quantified evidence found: Peveler 2005 above (about 70% inside 25 to 35 degrees, and significantly lower than Hamley). No other peer reviewed validation found.

## 2. KOPS (knee over pedal spindle) and critiques
- KOPS: tibial tuberosity plumb line over pedal spindle with crank horizontal. Keith Bontrager, "The Myth of KOPS": it has "no biomechanical basis at all"; the leg plus crank lever system works the same when rotated about the BB so gravity is not the relevant variable; recumbents disprove the necessity; he proposes fitting for weight distribution and balance across seated, climbing and sprinting, with most riders ending near 72 to 74.5 degree effective seat angles. https://www.sheldonbrown.com/kops.html
- Modern fitters still measure knee forward of foot (KFOF) at 3 o'clock as a reference. Retul includes KFOF in mm as one of its 7 most used variables (Branco 2022, below).

## 3. Joint angle ranges used by fitters

### 3.1 Knee at BDC: static vs dynamic targets
- Static (Holmes): 25 to 35 degrees flexion; Bini 2011 narrows to 25 to 30.
- Dynamic: Ferrer-Roca 2012 uses 30 to 40 degrees for 2D dynamic. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22190163/
- Swart and Holliday 2019 (Curr Sports Med Rep 18(12):490-6): "Optimal KFA using dynamic measurements should range from 33 to 43 degrees at low intensity to 30 to 40 degrees at high intensity when measured at the bottom dead center crank position"; also "Setting the saddle height using the Holmes static method is optimal for injury prevention and performance"; hip, shoulder and elbow "have not been investigated to any meaningful extent". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31834181/
- Retul ranges reported by third parties (not official docs): knee extension 30 to 40 degrees and knee flexion "slightly under 110 to 115 degrees", ankling max 95 to 105 degrees, knee lateral travel 40 mm per stroke as a red flag (CX Magazine 2010) https://www.cxmagazine.com/retul-cyclocross-bike-fitting ; BikeRadar: Retul average knee flexion 110 plus or minus 3, extension 37 plus or minus 3 https://www.bikeradar.com/reviews/maintenance/tools-kits-and-boxes/fitting-systems/retul-fit-system-review ; TriRig: knee flexion "to fall within 35 and 40 degrees" and "Many of the acceptable ranges in a Retul fit are only five degrees" https://tririg.com/blogs/latest/review-retul-university

### 3.2 Hip, torso, shoulder, elbow (Holliday and Swart 2021, PMC9219349, 50 well-trained male road cyclists, static inclinometer on own bike)
- Literature table in that paper: ankle 5 to 15 degrees plantarflexion at BDC (Burt 2014); hip 55 to 65 degrees at TDC (femur to trochanter to shoulder) road (Burt 2014); elbow 20 to 30 degrees on hoods (Burt 2014), 60 to 70 degrees in drops (Silberman 2005); torso 45 to 55 degrees recreational on hoods and 45 to 30 degrees fast road on hoods (Burt 2014); torso 45 degrees to a non-sloping top tube in drops (Silberman 2005); torso 30 to 45 degrees racing, 40 to 50 degrees fitness, 50 to 60 degrees casual on hoods (Pruitt and Matheny 2006). No shoulder range existed in the literature. No TT ranges in that paper.
- Measured means: knee 36 plus or minus 7 (range 20 to 51) at BDC; hip 77 plus or minus 5 (67 to 86) at TDC; shoulder 112 plus or minus 7 (99 to 129); elbow 19 plus or minus 8 (3 to 45); ankle 116 plus or minus 7. Authors estimate static and dynamic knee angles differ by about 8 degrees, so their 36 degree mean maps to roughly 28 degrees by the Holmes method. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9219349
- Reach and handlebar height formulas from arm and torso length are attributed to de Vey Mestdagh 1998 (Appl Ergon 29(5):325-34, "posture length" as well as "posture height"). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9703347/
- Reach and drop: no peer reviewed centimeter rule found. Practitioner guidance uses torso angle and elbow angle instead. Elbow of about 90 degrees on TT extensions is the common fitter convention (forum and coaching sources only, not a study).

### 3.3 Kinematics change with intensity
- Holliday 2023 (Sports Biomech 22(5):660-74), 17 cyclists at 60/80/90% HRmax: ankle dorsiflexion and knee extension increased 6 to 9% at higher intensity; elbow flexion increased 39% from 60% to 90%; lumbar and thoracic flexion increased 7%. Fit ranges should account for intensity. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31475880/
- Peveler 2012, 34 cyclists, 2D: plantar flexion increased from static to active pedaling, which increased knee angle (more extension); less plantar flexion at higher intensities; more variability in novices. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22158094/

## 4. Cleat fore-aft
- Traditional: first MTP (ball of foot) over pedal spindle. Van Sickle and Hull 2007 (J Biomech 40(6):1262-7), 11 competitive cyclists at about 90% VT, 90 rpm: VO2 not affected by forward vs mid vs rear (cleat halfway between first metatarsal head and heel) positions, group p = 0.311; VO2 2705, 2696, 2747 ml/min. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16901493/
- Paton 2009 (IJSPP 4(4):517-23), 10 competitive cyclists, crossover arch (midfoot) vs preferred: VO2max minus 0.5% (plus or minus 2.0), peak power minus 0.8% (plus or minus 1.3), steady state VO2 at 60% minus 2.4% (plus or minus 1.1), 30 min TT power minus 1.5% (plus or minus 2.9); "no substantial physiological or performance advantage". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20029102/
- Steve Hogg: uses midfoot himself, learned from Gotz Heine, does not push it on clients; cites Paton's null result and the Paton and Jardine duathlon follow up (about 30 s better over 5 km run after bike, rationale "unload the lower limbs (calf) musculature prior to the run"). https://www.stevehoggbikefitting.com/?p=2955 (his Footloose PDF is encrypted and could not be read.)

## 5. Crank length
- Martin and Spirduso 2001 (Eur J Appl Physiol 84(5):413-8), 16 trained cyclists, 120/145/170/195/220 mm: max power 1149 W (220 mm) to 1194 W (145 mm), about 4% spread over an 100 mm range; optimal cadence 136 rpm (120 mm) to 110 rpm (220 mm); optimal crank 20% of leg length or 41% of tibia length; "use of the standard 170 mm length cranks should not substantially compromise maximum power in most adults." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11417428/
- Macdermid and Edwards 2010 (Eur J Appl Physiol 108(1):177-82), 7 female XC MTB athletes, 170/172.5/175: time to peak power shorter with 170 (2.57 vs 3.29 s); no other differences in isokinetic power or endurance indices. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19771448/
- Ferrer-Roca 2017 (J Sports Sci 35(14):1328-35), 12 amateur road cyclists, preferred plus or minus 5 mm at 150/200/250 W: no change in HR or gross efficiency (20.4, 20.1, 20.3%); longer crank increased hip and knee max flexion and ROM by 1.8 to 3.4 degrees; "in case of doubt between two lengths, the shorter one might be recommended." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27484153/
- Barratt, Martin, Elmer, Korff 2016 (MSSE 48(4):705-13), 15 trained cyclists, 150 to 190 mm at 240 W 90 rpm: crank length caused large increases in knee and hip excursions; joint moments and powers largely maintained; knee extension power tended to fall and hip extension power rise with longer cranks. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26559455/

## 6. TT and triathlon: aero vs comfort, seat angle, rules
- Heil, Wilcox, Quinn 1995 (MSSE 27(5):730-5), 25 trained triathletes and cyclists, seat tube angles 69/76/83/90 with aero bars at about 73% VO2peak: VO2, HR, RPE at 83 and 90 degrees significantly lower than at 69 (3.09, 3.10 vs 3.17 L/min); 76, 83 and 90 similar; steeper STA produced greater hip extension and a limb orientation more directly over the crank. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7674878/
- Garside and Doran 2000 (J Sports Sci 18(10):825-33), 8 male triathletes, 40 km at about 70% VO2peak at 73 vs 81 degrees then 10 km run: run 42:55 vs 46:15 (p < 0.01); combined 1:45:49 vs 1:50:33; gains mostly in the first 5 km. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11055818/
- Fintelman 2015 (J Sports Sci 33(16):1730-7), 19 well-trained males at preferred, 0, 8, 16, 24 degree torso angles: all maximal variables decreased with lower torso angle (p < 0.001); 0 degrees significantly worse than all others. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25658151/
- Fintelman 2014 (J Biomech 47(8):1894-8): optimal torso angle falls as speed rises; aero losses outweigh power losses above 46 km/h; fully horizontal torso not optimal; below 30 km/h a more upright TT position is better. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24726654/
- UCI article 1.3.013: saddle peak at least 5 cm behind a vertical plane through the BB spindle; morphological exception lets it move forward to that plane but not past it; only one morphological exemption (saddle or extensions); track sprint, keirin, 500 m and 1 km exempt from the 5 cm rule. Read via https://www.wpcycling.com/track-news/uci-rule-1-3-013-and-1-3-022/ (quotes the regulation; not the UCI PDF itself).
- UCI TT changes effective 1 January 2023 (official press release): three height categories; BB axle to end of extensions max 80 cm (under 180 cm), 83 cm (180 to 189.9 cm), 85 cm (190 cm and over); max height difference forearm support to highest extension point under 10 cm, 12 cm, 14 cm; forearm support inclination max raised from 15 to 30 degrees. https://www.uci.org/pressrelease/uci-regulations-for-road-cycling-changes-for-time-trials/1QmN4gRxrsgeQtgahAAfXr
- AeroCoach "UCI Time Trial regulations for 2026 onwards" (dated 21 January 2026) lists max pad to shifter height 100/120/140 mm by category. http://www.aero-coach.co.uk/uci-time-trial-position-regulations-for-2026-onwards
- World Triathlon Competition Rules (edition dated 13 December 2025, approved January and March 2025), section 5.2: draft-illegal events, saddle front-most point no more than 5 cm in front of and no more than 15 cm behind the vertical through the chain wheel axle; draft-legal U23 and Elite, saddle front-most point no less than 5 cm (men) and 2 cm (women) behind that line; bike max 185 cm long, 50 cm wide, BB height 24 to 30 cm, front center 54 to 65 cm; UCI TT homologated bikes always allowed in draft-illegal events. PDF read from https://www.triathlonmanitoba.ca/wp-content/uploads/World-Triathlon_Competition-Rules_20260429.pdf (hosted copy of the World Triathlon document). Secondary confirmation: https://thetriathlete.co.uk/world-triathlon-competition-rules-2026-guide/
- Slowtwitch (Empfield) on why the UCI 5 cm rule penalizes short riders: with nose 5 cm behind BB, a 172 cm rider at 70 cm saddle height sits at about 74.5 degrees while a 188 cm rider at 80 cm sits at about 76.5 degrees. https://www.slowtwitch.com/industry/x-y-rules-in-an-angular-world/

## 7. Injury correlates
- Clarsen, Krosshaug, Bahr 2010 (AJSM 38(12):2494-501), 109 of 116 pros from 7 teams: 94 injuries, 45% lower back, 23% knee; 23 time-loss injuries, 57% knee, 22% lower back, 13% lower leg; 58% had low back pain in 12 months (41% sought medical attention); 36% anterior knee pain (19% sought attention). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20847225/
- Wilber 1995 (Int J Sports Med 16(3):201-6), 294 men + 224 women recreational: 85% at least one overuse complaint, 36% needing treatment; neck 48.8%, knees 41.7%, groin/buttocks 36.1%, hands 31.1%, back 30.3%; women 1.5x neck and 2.0x shoulder odds. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7649713/
- Dettori and Norvell 2006 (Sports Med 36(1):7-18): non-traumatic injury prevalence up to 85%; common sites knee, neck/shoulder, hands, buttock, perineum; prevention strategies "remain untested". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16445308/
- Knee pain mechanism: Bini 2011: patellofemoral compressive force inversely related to saddle height (low saddle, more anterior knee load). Secondary sources attribute to Holmes 1994 the pairing of low saddle with anterior knee pain and high saddle with posterior knee pain; the original was not reached. Asplund and St Pierre 2004 (Phys Sportsmed 32(4):23-30): anterior knee pain and PFPS among most common leg overuse injuries; bike fit, equipment change, training load and anatomy are the evaluation factors. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20086406/
- Low back pain: Streisfeld 2017 systematic review (Sports Health 9(1):75-79), 8 studies: LBP reported by more than half of cyclists; lower handlebar heights associated with increased lumbosacral flexion; LBP cyclists show core activation imbalances, extensor endurance deficits and increased lumbar flexion. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27784817/ Burnett 2004 pilot (Man Ther 9(4):211-9), 9 vs 9: pain group trended to more lower lumbar flexion and rotation with loss of multifidus co-contraction. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15522646/
- Hand numbness: Patterson 2003 (AJSM 31(4):585-9), 25 riders before and after a 600 km ride: 23 of 25 had motor or sensory symptoms; motor alone 36% of hands, sensory alone 10%, both 24%; recommends gloves, proper fit, changing hand position. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12860549/
- Perineal numbness: Sommer 2001 (Int J Sports Med 22(6):410-3), 40 men: penile pO2 fell significantly in over 70% while seated; 61% reported genital numbness; 19% of those riding over 400 km per week reported erectile dysfunction. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11531032/ Schwarzer 2002 (Eur Urol 41(2):139-43): saddle width, not padding, protects perfusion (pO2 drop 82.4% narrow padded vs 20.3% wide noseless). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12074400/ Schrader 2008 (J Sex Med 5(8):1932-40), 90 bicycle police: no-nose saddle cut perineal pressure 66%; riders free of urogenital paresthesia rose from 27% to 82%. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18466268/
- Women: Gaither 2018 (J Sex Med 15(4):510-8), 3118 women: cyclists vs non-cyclists OR for genital numbness 6.5 (low intensity) and 9.1 (high), saddle sores OR 6.3 and 22.7; no higher sexual dysfunction. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29548713/ Bury 2021 scoping review: limited evidence on saddle sore prevalence; handlebar position relative to saddle and reducing perineal pressure had some evidence. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33109466/ Olewinski 2026 (JSAMS, online), 33 elite/pro cyclists with vulvas: 91% had pain and abrasions; 42% said a prior bike fit contributed negatively to saddle health. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42786051/

## 8. Static vs dynamic measurement
- Fonda, Sarabon, Li 2014 (J Sports Sci 32(10):940-6), 11 cyclists: 3D, 2D high speed video and electrogoniometer all underestimated knee angle vs 3D (p = 0.00, eta squared 0.73); all dynamic methods differed from static (ES 0.4 to 0.6); good intra-session reliability; add 2.2 degrees to 2D values for precision. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24499342/
- Holliday, Fisher, Theo, Swart 2017 (Eur J Sport Sci 17(9):1129-42), 19 cyclists, 3 trials at 70% PPO: goniometer and inclinometer correlated for all joints; 3D motion capture correlated with them only at knee, shoulder, elbow; static to dynamic knee change is attributable to foot position and negated when controlled; "3DMC is not interchangeable with GM and IM" and needs its own reference values. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28749730/ Branco 2022 reports Holliday 2017 within-subject ICC for dynamic measures of 0.68 to 0.87.
- Holliday and Swart 2021 (PMC9219349): static vs dynamic knee angles differ by about 8 degrees per cited studies. Holliday and Swart 2022 (Phys Med Rehabil Clin N Am 33(1):1-13): static kinematics are "a valid and reliable tool" for the initial configuration, dynamic methods to fine tune. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34798992/
- Peveler 2012: static to dynamic increases plantar flexion and knee angle (see 3.3).
- Which targets are static vs dynamic: Holmes 25 to 35 is static with pedal at 6 o'clock. Ferrer-Roca 30 to 40, Swart 2019 30 to 43 and the Retul 30 to 40 extension range are dynamic (max extension, not necessarily BDC). Priego Quesada 2017: 30 degrees "from the offset position" equals about 40 degrees absolute in 2D.

## 9. Repeatability and inter-rater reliability
- Branco, Resende, Carpes, Mendonca 2022, "Does the Retul System provide reliable kinematics information for cycling analysis?", Journal of Science and Cycling, open access: 10 Brazilian Retul fitters surveyed (47.6% response) to pick the 7 most used variables (ankle max and min, max knee flexion, max knee extension, back angle, knee forward of foot, shoulder angle to wrist); 10 cyclists measured, 3 day interval; ICC3,3: AMAX 0.87, AMIN 0.86, MAXKF 0.87, MAXKE 0.98, BA 0.99, KFOF 0.83, SATW 0.97; SEM under 3 degrees for all angles and 4.3 mm for KFOF; MDD 2.15 to 6.55 degrees and 15.51 mm. The paper's wording mixes "inter-tester" and "intra-examiner"; the design reads as repeated sessions by trained examiners. Retul Vantage samples at 18 Hz. https://repositorio.ufmg.br/bitstream/1843/65097/2/Does%20the%20Ret%c3%bcl%20System%20provide%20reliable%20kinematics%20information%20for%20cycling%20analysis_.pdf
- Fonda 2014: intra-session ICC above 0.94 for dynamic methods (as cited by Branco). Holliday 2017: all three instruments reliable; ICC 0.68 to 0.87 for dynamic (as cited by Branco).
- No study of agreement between independent human fitters (true inter-rater) was found within the search budget. Open question.

## 10. How the commercial systems structure a fit and what they output
- Retul (Specialized): official page lists 4 steps: pre-fit physical assessment (limitations, injuries, goals); 3D motion capture with LED markers on 8 anatomical points via the Vantage system; adjustments plus equipment recommendations (shoes, footbeds, saddle, bars); Zin tool digital map of the final bike, compiled into a report. https://www.retul.com/retul-fit Dealer pages: Zin touches 13 to 15 points on saddle, bars and frame; data captured per side at increasing wattage; rider gets digital file and printout (BikeRadar). Sweet Pete's: Dynamic Fit 1.5 to 2.5 h, USD 350; combo with pressure mapping 2 to 2.5 h, USD 400; output described as a cloud saved record of final bike measurements and 3D body angles. https://www.sweetpetes.com/about/fitting-pg127.htm ; https://www.landrys.com/pages/retul-bike-fit-at-landrys
- Specialized Body Geometry Fit (dealer description, Landis Cyclery): pre-fit interview; 23-step flexibility and structural assessment (foot structure, spinal curvature, hip flexibility, leg length discrepancy, glute and core strength); side view analysis (cleat, saddle, stem, bar in that order; hip and torso angle, shoulder, elbow, hand alignment); front view analysis (hip, knee, foot alignment, squareness on saddle, footbeds, forefoot shims, cleat wedges); follow up about a week later. https://landiscyclery.com/about/specialized-body-geometry-fit-pg349.htm
- Trek Precision Fit (AeroGeeks review): interview; off-bike physical assessment of body morphology in eight areas plus ROM, flexibility, plank test; shoe and cleat setup; sit bone measurement on pressure board, saddle height and setback on a Purely Custom fit bike; 2D camera motion capture compared against angular norms; upper body (stem, pads, extensions) with power meter. https://aerogeeks.com/?p=5352 Trek-Segafredo team process (Trek Racing): history, flexibility and strength, structural asymmetry, foot evaluation, multi-planar high speed video, pressure mapping in saddle and shoes, no changes until about an hour in, velodrome aero testing for TT athletes. https://racing.trekbikes.com/stories/equipment/how-trek-segafredo-does-bike-fitting
- BikeFit (Paul Swift): founded 1995 after co-developing the Cleat Wedge; Swift was 8-time US national champion; Fit Kit (1980s) was the first modern system, BikeFit the second and first to focus on the foot-pedal interface; CyclePoint (2018, Spokane) is the exclusive education provider with Level 1 (foot-pedal interface and contact points) and Level 2 courses, and partners with Slowtwitch F.I.S.T.; products include cleat wedges, leg length shims, sizing stem, Saddle Changer. https://bikebiz.com/?p=23266 ; https://bicycleretailer.com/industry-news/2018/02/13/paul-swift-opens-fitting-education-company-cyclepoint
- IBFI (International Bike Fitting Institute): not for profit body formed from the 2014 and 2015 International CycleFit Symposium; four certification levels by credits (Level 1 = 120 credits, doubling per level) across courses, conferences, technology, CPD and academia; system agnostic; about 60 certified fitters at the 2017 interview; later syllabus splits each level into an online and an in-person module, Level 1 covering anatomy, pre-fit interview and pre-fit screening. IBFI does not publish a standard session protocol. https://cyclingindustry.news/ibfi-interview-where-does-the-industry-stand-with-bike-fitting-gone-bad-claims

## 11. Implications for a phone camera app
- Measure dynamically at cadence, at an intensity near what the rider trains at (Swart 2019, Holliday 2023). Report knee extension at max extension and label whether the value is static or dynamic, 2D or 3D, with the frame of reference, because the same rider reads 25 to 30 static and 33 to 43 dynamic.
- 2D side-view video underestimates knee angle vs 3D by about 2 degrees (Fonda). An app should state measurement uncertainty; single-camera SEM is unlikely to beat the 2 to 3 degree SEM of Retul.
- Saddle height changes under 4% of leg length (about 3 cm) have trivial physiological effect; only recommend changes larger than measurement noise (MDD 2 to 6 degrees).
- Hip angle at TDC and TT specific joint targets have no peer reviewed reference ranges; show them descriptively, not as pass or fail.
- Comfort and injury: anterior knee pain correlates with low saddle (higher patellofemoral force), low back pain with low bars and lumbar flexion, hand numbness with weight on hands, perineal numbness with saddle width and nose pressure. Rule and discipline awareness: UCI 5 cm setback vs World Triathlon minus 5 to plus 15 cm.
